For the last five weeks we've taken the five questions one at a time. This week, the method: running all five on one trade idea, in order, in a single sitting.

Taken separately, each question catches something. Run together, they do something more useful. Each answer feeds the next, and by the end you have either a plan you can defend or a clear reason to leave the idea alone.

"Here's my trade. Stress-test it."

That gets you a bit of everything and not much of anything. A few risks, a caveat about volatility, a reminder to use a stop. The AI decides what a stress test is, and it usually decides on the gentle version.

Order matters

The five questions aren't a menu. They run from outside your idea to inside your plan:

  1. The strongest argument against. Start with the opponent, before you've grown attached to the details.
  2. The assumptions. Now look at what your case quietly relies on.
  3. The contradicting evidence. Check those assumptions against what is already in front of you.
  4. What you're overlooking. Step outside the frame you chose.
  5. What would invalidate it. Turn everything above into conditions you can act on once you're in.

Run them the other way round and you tend to write the invalidation first, then spend the other four questions defending it.

A prompt that does the job

You can run the five as separate messages, which is slower and usually sharper. If you want a single pass, use this:

"Here is my trade idea: [instrument, direction, entry, stop, target, and timeframe]. My thesis: [two or three sentences]. What I looked at: [your analysis]. Other open positions: [list, or none]. Work through these five steps in order, and keep every answer specific to this setup. 1. Taking the opposite position, give the single strongest argument against this trade. 2. List the assumptions my thesis depends on, and mark the one that matters most. 3. From what I've given you, what already contradicts my thesis? 4. What should I check that I haven't, under these headings: scheduled events, overlap with other positions, costs, other timeframes, and practical constraints? 5. Give three observable conditions, separate from my stop, that would show the thesis is wrong. Don't tell me whether to take the trade. Don't state current dates or figures as fact."

The last two sentences carry a lot of weight. An AI coach left to its own devices will finish with a verdict, and a verdict is the one thing you didn't ask for.

What to do with the answer

Don't read it as a report. Treat it as a sheet with five boxes, and write one line of your own in each:

  1. Your answer to the counter-argument, or an honest "I can't answer this".
  2. The assumption you'd least like to be wrong about, and how you'll know if you are.
  3. The contradiction you're choosing to accept, and why.
  4. Anything from the checks that changed the plan.
  5. Your invalidation conditions, written next to your stop.

Then count the boxes you couldn't fill. None empty: carry on with your normal process. One empty: that's a gap you're carrying, and it should count against your size. Two or more: you aren't ready. Wait, pass, or run it on a demo account first.

The whole thing takes ten or fifteen minutes. That feels long the first few times. It gets shorter, because the same gaps keep turning up, and once you've seen them often enough you start closing them before you ask.

Keep the five lines with the trade in your journal. When you review it later, they tell you what you knew going in, which is a fairer test of the decision than the P&L.
