Four questions in, AI has argued against your trade, surfaced its assumptions, listed what already contradicts it, and pointed at what you weren't looking at. This week, the last of the five:

What would invalidate my thesis?

The first four questions test the idea before you act. This one sets the terms for the moment after you act, when you're least able to judge it fairly.

"Where should I put my stop?"

It's a reasonable question, and you'll get a reasonable answer: below the recent low, beyond the range, a multiple of average volatility. But it answers a different question. A stop is where you get out if price goes against you. It says nothing about whether the reason you got in is still true.

A stop is a price. An invalidation is a reason.

Take a hypothetical short on a stock index after it fails at a level where it turned lower before. Your thesis is that sellers are still active there. Your stop sits a little above the level.

Now suppose the index drifts sideways for a week, just under your entry. The stop is never touched. But each day it holds near the level, the idea that sellers are defending it gets weaker. Price hasn't proved you wrong. The behaviour has.

Without a written invalidation, you'll keep holding, because nothing has told you to leave. With one, you'd have said in advance: "If it hasn't moved away from the level within three sessions, the thesis has failed, whatever the P&L says."

That's the difference. A stop protects your account. An invalidation protects your judgement.

A prompt that does the job

"Here is my trade idea: [instrument, direction, entry, stop, target, and timeframe]. My thesis: [two or three sentences on why]. Give me three specific, observable conditions that would show my thesis is wrong, separate from my stop being hit. Include at least one based on time and at least one based on something other than price. For each, tell me how I would recognise it on my own screen. Don't tell me whether the trade is good."

Three things make it work:

  1. It separates the reason from the stop. Without that line, an AI coach will hand you stop-placement advice and call it invalidation.
  2. It asks for time and non-price conditions. Those are the ones traders skip, and the ones that catch slow failures.
  3. It asks how you'd recognise each one. A condition you can't observe isn't a condition. It's a mood.

What to do with the answer

Rewrite each condition in your own words, then sort them:

  1. Clear and checkable. Write it into your plan as an exit rule, next to the stop. If it happens, you leave, at whatever price that turns out to be.
  2. Sensible but vague. Tighten it until a stranger could tell whether it had happened. "Momentum fades" becomes "two closes back inside the range".
  3. Nothing you can name. If neither you nor the AI can say what would prove the idea wrong, the idea can't be tested. Pass, or take it on a demo account until you can.

Then do the uncomfortable part. When a condition triggers, act on it, even if the position is showing a profit. The thesis was the reason for the trade. When it goes, so does the reason.

Your review gets easier, too. Instead of asking whether the trade worked, you can ask whether you left when you said you would.
